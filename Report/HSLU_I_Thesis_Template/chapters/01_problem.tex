\chapter{Problem, Research Question, Vision}
The Ascento Guard is an autonomous security robot designed to patrol large outdoor areas across a range of customer domains 
including warehouses, industrial manufacturing sites, airports, and datacentres. The platform navigates uneven 
terrain via a legged locomotion system and operates continuously through autonomous charging, without human 
intervention. During its patrols with pre-defined POI's four cameras creating a 360° view are used for event 
detection. The detection capabilities span a broad operational mandate: identifying unauthorized persons on 
permises, verifying perimeter integrity, monitoring doors, windows, lights, and controlling parking lots.


\section{Problem}
As Ascento's customer base grows, so does the specificity of detection requirements. 
Each new site may introduce new object classes or event types that are not covered by any existing 
pretrained model and that occur too infrequently - if ever - to accumulate usable training data organically 
during robot patrols to train models.
This produces two interdependent data problems:
The first is the zero-shot case: If an event has never been recorded in a form suitable for training, 
no supervised model can be built from it directly.
The second is the sparse-data case: even if an event has been observed, available recordings are insufficient 
in volume for stable detection training.
In order to satisfy growing customer requests, Ascento has to come up with a way to create datasets for detection 
training, without already owning organic data.
Generative AI appears as a natural candidate to start solving the zero-shot case,  but introduces its own failure 
modes in this context. Unrestricted synthesis produces images with AI artefacts, physically implausible 
configurations, and a domain gap relative to the robot's actual camera images. The model has no grounding in 
the physical world as seen from fixed height and it provides no mechanism to enforce a realistic appearance. 
Additionally, web-scale image datasets such as \gls{laion}\cite{laion} or ImageNet\cite{image-net}, 
which comprise the basis of many generative model training data, are dominated by intentional photography in which 
the subject is deliberately centered and staged, causing generative models to reproduce this compositional bias. 
The Ascento Guard's patrol images capture objects of interest incidentally: they may appear in the periphery, 
partially occluded, or at a small scale relative to the frame.
These failure modes are structural properties of text-only conditioning that make naïve diffusion synthesis 
insufficient for downstream detection training without additional control during the generation process.



Handling the sparse-data case introduces a different constraint. When a small set of real recordings exists, 
classical augmentation, such as geometric transforms, colour jitter, or cutout mosaic, can be used to increase the effective dataset size. 
However, all of the augmented data is based on the original instances. To avoid biasing the model towards these instances, 
and to keep the possibility for generalization on unseen data intanct, only a limited amount of augmented data can be injected 
into the original dataset, to the magnitude of only a few percent. 

The combined effect of the above described constraints of generative diffusion and classical augmentation mean that neither approach 
out of the box is a viable path to solve the rare or novel event detection problem for the Ascento Guard. 
A more controlled data synthetization and augmentation pipeline is required. One that can produce physically 
plausible, domain-consistent training images without depending solely on real incident recordings. 

\section{Research Question}
The central question is whether synthetic data, generated through a controlled pipeline combining guided diffusion and object inpainting, 
can close the detection gap for rare-event classes where real data is absent or insufficient. This decomposes into three concrete sub-questions:

\resq{1}{Does the proposed pipeline improve detection performance over a baseline trained without synthetic data?.}
\resq{2}{What is the relative contribution of different augmentation strategies to final detection performance?}
\resq{3}{Does the pipeline generalize to other usecases?}

\section{Vision}
The target is a \gls{yolo} detector exceeding \gls{map@50} **(reference chapter 6)** of 0.75 on the relevant class. 
False negatives are the primary optimization concern: a missed detection is the worst-case outcome in 
this deployment context. False positives are less harmful as they are caught by downstream human supervision. 
The data generation pipeline is validated on one distinct usecase, and the engineering effort to add a second usecase will be evaluated. 
Out of scope are hardware integration, real-world deployment verification on customer sites, and on-robot inference testing. 
Questions regarding minimum viable synthetic count per class and human-in-the-loop verification design are identified as open problems 
but not addressed within this work.
